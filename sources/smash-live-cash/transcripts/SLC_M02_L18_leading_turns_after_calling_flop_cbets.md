## [00:00] Why build turn leads?

After check-calling the flop, OOP can lead turns to force value into the pot, deny free cards and pressure unpaired hands that struggle to defend.

## [01:55] Three families of lead spots

Nick identifies:

1. low turns after IP's high-card-heavy c-bet;
2. paired second- or bottom-pair turns after a polar big bet;
3. paired-board turns where vulnerable top pair can value/protection bet.

## [03:50] Three example boards

The examples are 9-5-4 two-tone, J-8-3 rainbow and K-Q-2 two-tone, all at deep cash-game depth.

## [05:45] 9-5-4: IP's polar c-bet

HJ's large c-bet contains overpairs plus high-card and draw bluffs. BB mostly calls rather than raises, preserving top pair and many draws.

## [07:40] Low turns improve OOP's relative position

On low cards that miss IP's high cards, BB's equity rises and leading becomes available. Paired 4/5 turns and straight-completing cards are especially intuitive candidates.

## [09:35] Lead value and bluff construction

Top pair, two pair, straights and selected draws lead. Low cards and draws that avoid blocking IP's air support the bluff side.

## [11:30] Defence against the lead

IP is supposed to call and raise with difficult high-card and pair categories. Nick expects many live players to miss these responses.

## [12:30] Brick turns can favour OOP after a large c-bet

The recovered transition clarifies that OOP should also look beyond obvious paired or straight-completing turns. On a low board, a nominal brick can be very good for BB when:

- BB's large-bet calling range has been cleared of weak high-card hands;
- BB remains pair- and equity-driven;
- IP's polar flop bet still contains substantial unpaired high cards that did not improve.

The lead is driven by the relative range change, not by the turn card looking dramatic.

## [13:20] One board represents a wider low-board family

The 9-5-4 example is intended to generalise to related 7-, 8- and 9-high boards. The practical question is what IP's flop c-bet composition does when the board remains low.

After a large c-bet and call, low turns can increase OOP's relative equity because IP retains total-air high-card hands while OOP no longer contains much unpaired dust.

## [14:35] Transition to J-8-3: wide small bets create a different lead range

On J-8-3, IP c-bets close to range and therefore retains more pair and value classes—not only a polar overpair/high-card structure. BB also retains many top- and second-pair hands after calling.

This changes the turn-leading mechanism. Paired 8/3 turns can support small, linear value/protection leads rather than the more polar pressure used after a large c-bet.

## [15:30] Top-pair retention matters

BB did not remove all strong Jx through flop check-raises, so the turn range remains protected by top pair. This permits a broader small lead when lower board cards pair.

## [16:33] Small lead forces difficult high-card defence

The lead is priced to value-bet weak pairs while forcing IP to continue with KQ, ace-high and other non-obvious hands. Missing raises and calls make the lead overperform.

## [18:28] Compare the two lead mechanisms

- 9-5-4 attacks a polar, high-card-heavy large c-bet on favourable low turns.
- J-8-3 uses small linear value/protection leads after a wide small c-bet.

## [20:23] Transition to node-locked K-Q-2

The final example introduces a large c-bet that humans may construct too polar, creating additional leads when second pair pairs.
