## [00:00] Preflop master sheet and baseline philosophy

Nick introduces a preflop master sheet built from equilibrium ranges. The charts are not intended to be followed religiously. Their purpose is to provide the baseline shape for each position, stack depth and game setup so that later deviations are deliberate rather than improvised.

Examples of legitimate deviations include opening wider with a weak blind behind or tightening with aggressive regulars behind. The repeated methodological point is that a player needs to know the baseline before knowing where and how to deviate.

## [02:00] Rake and no-rake chart families

The master sheet contains both rake and no-rake ranges. Rake pushes strategy tighter and more aggressive because entering the pot incurs an additional cost. The course mainly uses no-rake ranges for its postflop work because many live populations play substantially looser than strict high-rake equilibrium ranges.

Nick does not say that rake can be ignored. He notes that the rake charts may be the better baseline in genuinely high-rake games, depending on how opponents adapt.

## [03:30] No-ante games are tighter and more aggressive

With no ante, less dead money is available. Opens, flats and blind defences therefore become tighter. Flatting represents a smaller proportion of the range and more continuing hands move toward 3-bet-or-fold structures.

The source contrasts this with ante and tournament environments, where the better price supports wider participation.

## [05:00] The hidden cost of being slightly too loose

Loose opens and defends often create small, emotionally invisible losses rather than memorable stack-offs. A player may defend a weak offsuit hand, check-fold the flop and immediately forget the lost blind. Across thousands of hands, these fractional mistakes can become expensive.

A soft table can justify widening when implied profit is real, but `the game is soft` is not sufficient evidence by itself. The range should be shifted consciously from a baseline.

## [07:30] Charts are shapes, while preflop remains memorisation-heavy

Nick rejects subjective `loose-game chart` and `tight-game chart` families because they would be too vague. Equilibrium charts provide one stable reference, while opponent-specific deviations remain the player's responsibility.

Preflop is described as one of the parts of poker that must be known relatively cold. This is not only for preflop execution: every postflop node begins as a range-versus-range problem generated by the preflop actions.

## [09:00] Read range composition through high-weight combinations

When estimating a range, Nick prioritises the combinations that make up most of its weight. In many ordinary spots these are:

- offsuit broadway and high-card combinations;
- pocket pairs;
- only then the smaller suited regions.

For a deep cold-call pot, he recommends asking which offsuit hands and pocket pairs the caller flats and which strong hands the caller would normally 3-bet. This composition helps determine which boards favour each player.

## [11:00] Preflop knowledge powers later deductive reasoning

The chart is not valuable merely because it produces a technically correct VPIP. It supplies the starting distribution for flop, turn and river reasoning. Without knowing what entered preflop, later blocker, bluff and board-ownership analysis becomes guesswork.

## [12:00] The offsuit-pip heuristic for preflop bluffs

Nick gives a practical method for finding polar 3-bet candidates when the exact chart is not memorised:

1. identify the weakest offsuit region the opener raises consistently;
2. prefer bluff candidates at or below that rank region;
3. favour hands that fold out dominating offsuit hands;
4. avoid candidates that block the folds they are trying to generate;
5. as stacks deepen, prefer suited playability over marginal offsuit material.

The spoken examples describe later positions opening progressively weaker offsuit kickers. Exact position-by-position boundaries remain visual- and chart-dependent.

Early-position no-ante ranges can look uncomfortably tight. Nick argues that these outputs assume theoretically strong play behind. Before widening, inspect what the actual players behind do:

## [19:00] Why the study sizes are not copied from oversized live opens

Live players often use larger open sizes with ranges that are as wide as, or wider than, ranges theoretically associated with smaller sizes. Solving against every oversized live raise literally would produce very tight and less useful reference charts.

The master sheet therefore uses standardised sizes intended to preserve realistic strategic shapes. In play, the student is expected to adjust to the actual size and opponent rather than treat the reference number as mandatory.

## [27:00] Weight or reach through later nodes

A hand may enter the 3-bet range at very low frequency but become a frequent bluff after it reaches a 4-bet node. Nick calls this its `weight` or `reach`: how often the hand arrives at a particular branch.

This prevents a common chart-reading error. A hand shown as a frequent action inside a late node may still represent very few total combinations because it rarely took the earlier action.

## [28:30] Five-bet bluffs depend on the opponent's 4-bet response

The goal is not to create mandatory five-bet bluffs. It is to prevent opponents from freely 4-bet/folding while preserving payment for the premium region when balance is needed.
