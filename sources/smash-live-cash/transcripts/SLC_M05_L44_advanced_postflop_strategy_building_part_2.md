## [25:19] Improvement value and check-back EV

Nick compares high-card and pocket-pair classes by their ability to improve and win large pots on later streets. A hand with lower immediate showdown value can have higher EV when its improvement creates stronger, more profitable river outcomes.

The source warns against judging a flop action only by current equity.

## [26:30] IP should preserve hands that can withstand turn pressure

Brad asks why hands such as suited connectors do not always c-bet simply because they have many natural barrel cards.

Nick explains that position needs a protected check-back range containing hands that can:

- turn straight draws and strong continues;
- call a turn overbet after flop checks through;
- realise equity on dynamic runouts.

Hands with less future resilience may c-bet more often even when their rank appears higher.

## [28:00] Do not strip the check-back range of future continues

If IP bets every low connected hand with obvious barrel potential, the check-back range reaches many turns without open-enders, strong draws or robust calls. The result is an exploitable check-back branch.

The lesson therefore distributes some strong-improvement hands into check while betting more of the hands that otherwise face mandatory turn folds.

## [30:00] Low-equity flop bets can be required for future bluff supply

After a small flop bet and call, OOP retains many high-card and medium-strength hands. IP does not need high immediate equity for every turn barrel. Some no-equity or low-equity hands must enter the aggressive branch because they:

- target the high-card call region;
- block strong ace-x or pair continues;
- supply bluffs on later blank and flush-completing rivers.

## [31:00] Missing difficult turn bluffs empties the river range

Brad acknowledges that he underuses hands such as weak Jx or low-card air as turn bluffs. Nick traces the consequence forward: without those candidates, IP reaches many rivers with no credible bluff inventory and an attentive opponent can overfold.

This is a direct example of range ancestry:

`flop bet → turn barrel candidate → river bluff availability`.

## [32:00] Showdown value and target-fold blockers choose the barrel

Hands such as Jx may barrel more than stronger Kx or Qx because:

- they block the defender's strongest ace-x kickers and pair continues;
- they can fold out hands that dominate them;
- stronger high-card hands retain more showdown value and can check.

The correct candidate is not simply the lowest-equity hand. It must interact with the range that called the flop.

## [34:00] River bluff selection on a completed front-door draw

On a river completing the front-door flush, Nick strongly dislikes using hands containing the completed suit as bluffs when the defender's missed flush draws are a major fold target.

The preferred bluff often:

- does not block the missed draw;
- blocks value or strong bluff-catcher classes;
- descended from a legitimate low-equity turn barrel.

## [35:30] Tough opponents punish missing bluff ancestry

In difficult games, sharp opponents can recognise that a player never reaches the river with the necessary one-suit or low-card turn barrels. They can then overfold the river even without observing many showdowns.

The lesson distinguishes population exploit from technical growth: underbluffing may survive in soft lineups, but a player who wants to beat tougher games must build the missing branches.

## [36:30] No-spade and backdoor-suit candidates

The recovered example prefers hands without the front-door suit on a blank-to-flush runout because they allow the opponent to hold and fold missed flush draws. Other suits and no-backdoor combinations can become better bluff candidates precisely because they do not remove the folds.

# Explicit Instructor Mechanisms

- Preserve future-call and improvement hands in the IP check-back range.
- Bet some hands because they cannot withstand turn pressure after checking.
- Low-equity turn barrels are required to seed later bluff inventory.
- Select barrels by target-fold and strongest-continue blockers, not rank alone.
- On missed-draw rivers, avoid blocking the missed draws intended to fold.
- Tough opponents can exploit missing bluff ancestry even without many showdowns.
