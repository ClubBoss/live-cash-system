## [00:00] Exploiting price inelasticity multiway

The lesson studies a dry paired ace-high multiway flop. A very small bet can overperform when live players react mainly to the existence of a bet rather than to the price they receive.

The exploit can be used with:

- air that wants to clear the field cheaply;
- thin value and protection hands;
- equity-driven backdoors that benefit from over-realisation.

## [02:00] Correct defence is much wider than population defence

Theoretical opponents must continue many high-card, pocket-pair and backdoor hands against a tiny bet. Live players often fold those classes because they evaluate only relative hand strength or are not paying attention to the exact size.

## [04:30] Do not pay more for the same fold

If 12% pot and 33% pot generate nearly the same folds, the smaller size wins the same immediate pot at much lower risk. A larger bet may even help an inattentive opponent play better by making the continue/fold boundary feel more obvious.

## [07:10] Recovered size-inelasticity explanation

Nick explicitly allows different exploit sizes by hand class against opponents unlikely to punish the imbalance:

- tiny with air and thin protection;
- a larger improvised size with very strong value;
- no requirement to mirror the exact solver menu when the opponent is not responding to it.

This is opponent-specific and should not be used transparently against attentive players.

## [09:00] Multiway bluffs should retain backup equity

Even when the population overfolds, Nick prefers extending the tiny-bet range with hands that have some future utility:

- two overcards against pocket pairs;
- backdoor straight or flush potential;
- interaction with likely folds.

Pure air without backup is less attractive because the pot remains multiway and raises are still possible.

## [10:00] A second source of over-realisation: under-raising

The exploit gains not only from excess folds but also from opponents failing to build the required raise range. Theory mixes raises from:

- strong Ax and value;
- pocket pairs and low-card interaction;
- backdoor broadways and matched blocker candidates.

Live opponents often call the obvious hands and under-raise the difficult bluffs and thin protection hands, allowing the bettor to realise more equity than expected.

## [12:00] Clearing the in-position player changes the BB node

When the tiny bet removes the player in between, BB reaches a heads-up continuation decision. Population may:

- fold too many backdoors;
- raise obvious strong Ax more often than theory;
- reveal hand strength through an over-linear raise range.

This gives the bettor additional visibility for later streets.

## [14:30] Why the cold-caller's range is strong on the paired ace-high board

The in-position cold-caller has a tighter preflop range than the opener but retains many pairs, offsuit Ax and suited aces. On the paired ace-high board, that range contains a high proportion of trips and pair-plus hands.

The initial opener therefore checks heavily at equilibrium. The strategic reason is range composition, not a generic rule that initial raisers must be passive multiway.

## [17:00] Small stab after the opener checks

After the initial raiser checks, the in-position player can use the tiny bet to pressure the BB while retaining a strong, condensed value region. The defender is required to continue extremely widely, which creates the same population overfold opportunity.

## [20:00] Do not assume tiny bets are always weak or always polar

The lesson distinguishes several functions:

- cheap air and protection;
- linear thin value;
- strong value using a separate exploit size;
- range-clearing in a multiway structure.

The correct interpretation depends on who bet, which player remains behind and what preflop range each seat carries.

## [22:00] Multiway check-raises are suppressed by an uncapped player behind

BB cannot build the same bluff check-raise range used heads-up while the initial raiser remains behind with an uncapped checking range. A bluff raise can be punished when that player wakes up with strong Ax.

The lesson therefore treats the player behind as a structural constraint, not merely another source of equity.

## [25:00] Delayed aggression after the field clears

Once the initial raiser folds and the pot becomes heads-up, BB can recover aggression on the turn. Hands that might have check-raised heads-up on the flop—strong Ax, low-card value, selected draws and blocker candidates—can appear in a turn-leading range instead.

## [28:00] Turn leads inherit the missing flop check-raise shape

The turn lead is built around:

- low Ax that unblocks pocket-pair calls;
- strongest Ax and value;
- low-card bluffs that pressure the in-position player's high-card stabs;
- selected thin protection hands.

The mechanism is delayed range expression: the multiway player behind prevented the flop raise, so some of that strategic shape appears after the field clears.

## [30:00] Lead candidates target the actual flop stab range

After the tiny flop stab, the in-position range contains many high-card and pocket-pair continues. Low-card turn leads can pressure the weak high-card region while extracting value and protection with small pairs.

The exact bluff/value map depends on which hands the in-position player stabbed and called.

## [32:00] Population under-barrelling strengthens the value lead

Theory requires the in-position player to keep bluffing many high-card and low-equity hands on neutral turns. Nick expects many live players to underdo those barrels.

When expected aggression will not arrive, BB should search for ways to put money in with value rather than wait for a bet that is unlikely to come.

## [34:00] Bluff raises remain dangerous while value expansion is safer

Nick remains cautious about adding flop bluff check-raises into an uncapped player behind. The downside of an imprecise bluff range is large. By contrast, expanding value raises or turn leads is less costly until opponents begin making extreme folds.

## [36:00] Practical turn-leading exploit

If the in-position player knows how to stab high-card air on the flop but does not continue it on neutral low turns, BB can lead the bottom of the range more aggressively. The small lead clears high-card hands that theory should often continue.

## [37:00–42:37] Recovered original tail and conclusion

The original non-looping tail continues the same themes:

- strong value may be raised when opponents will not find extreme folds;
- random flop bluff raises are expensive multiway;
- neutral low turns permit selective overbluffing after the field clears;
- tiny bets and small leads succeed because populations fold too much and raise too little.
